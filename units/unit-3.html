03 First Ionisation Energy

Defining IE₁

Definition · First Ionisation Energy (IE₁)

The minimum energy required to remove 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms to form 1 mol of gaseous ions.

The general equation:

General equation for first ionisation energy

For example, removing the first electron from a gaseous sodium atom requires +496 kJ/mol:

Example · First IE of Na

Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻

→
IE₁ = +496 kJ/mol
This removes the first electron from the outermost shell — in Na, that's the lone 3s¹ electron.
04 Second Ionisation Energy

Defining IE₂ — and why it's always higher

Definition · Second Ionisation Energy (IE₂)

The energy required to remove 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of unipositive (+1) gaseous ions.

General equation for second ionisation energy

For sodium: Na⁺(g) → Na²⁺(g) + e⁻ requires +4562 kJ/mol — nearly 10 times more than IE₁. Why such a huge jump? You're now removing an electron from the inner shell (the 2p⁶ shell), which is much closer to the nucleus and much harder to remove.

The number of IEs depends on the number of electrons in the atom. Sodium has 11 electrons, so it has a maximum of 11 successive ionisation energies — IE₁ through IE₁₁.

06 IE Across a Period

First IE increases across a period

Across a period, the first ionisation energy generally increases. The PSPM-style explanation uses this exact reasoning chain:

PSPM Reasoning Chain · IE Across a Period

Why IE increases from Na to Ar

01
Atomic size smaller due to proton number increases.
02
Number of inner electrons remains the same.
03
Effective nuclear charge increases.
04
Attraction of nucleus toward valence electron stronger.
05
More energy needed to remove outer electrons.
06
Ionisation energy increases across a period.
07 The Anomalies

The two anomalies across Period 3

The general trend is "IE increases across the period" — but there are two famous exceptions where IE actually drops instead of rising. Both happen because of the stability rule from Topic 2: fully filled and half filled are more stable than partially filled.

Anomaly 1 · Between Mg (Group 2) and Al (Group 13)

Mg has a fully filled 3s² orbital (configuration: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s²). Al has a partially filled 3p¹ orbital (configuration: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p¹). More energy is needed (IE higher) to remove the 1st electron from Mg because the fully filled 3s orbital is more stable than the partially filled 3p orbital in Al. Moreover, the 1st electron in the 3p orbital of Al is higher in energy, hence less stable and is easier to be removed, so its IE is lower.

Anomaly 2 · Between P (Group 15) and S (Group 16)

In P, Group 15 (3s² 3p³), all 3 electrons in the 3p orbital are single in the same spin — more stable (half filled 3p orbitals). In S, Group 16 (3s² 3p⁴), the 3p orbital is partially filled — less stable. Removing the outer electron from a partially filled orbital is much easier than from a half filled orbital. Less energy is needed to remove the 1st outer electron in sulphur. Therefore, the 1st IE in P is higher than S.

Memorise this

The same two anomalies happen in Period 2 (between Be–B and N–O) — the reasoning is identical. Anytime you see Group 2/13 or Group 15/16 in a question, expect to invoke the stability rule.

11 Successive IE

Successive ionisation energies — and why they always increase

For any atom, the successive ionisation energies always increase in this order:

IE₁ < IE₂ < IE₃ < IE₄ < IE₅ < IE₆ ...

Why IE always increases

The general reasoning

01
When an outer electron is removed from a neutral atom, the number of electrons decreases.
02
The repulsion among the remaining electrons decreases.
03
Attraction of valence electrons toward the nucleus getting stronger.
04
More energy is required to remove another electron from the positively charged ion.
But the increase isn't smooth

Energy required to remove each electron from the valence shell keeps increasing. However, this increase is not smooth. There is a sudden/drastic increase of IE when there is a change of shell — when electrons are removed from a shell that gets closer to the nucleus.

13 Deducing Group from IE

Worked example: finding the group

An element has the following successive ionisation energies (kJ/mol): 1012, 1903, 2910, 4956, 6278, 22230. Deduce its group number and write its valence electron configuration.

Method · Two ways to find the jump

Method 1: Differences between consecutive IEs

→
IE₂ − IE₁ = 891
IE₃ − IE₂ = 1007
IE₄ − IE₃ = 2046
IE₅ − IE₄ = 1322
IE₆ − IE₅ = 15952 ← largest difference

Method 2: Ratios between consecutive IEs

→
IE₂ / IE₁ = 1.88
IE₃ / IE₂ = 1.53
IE₄ / IE₃ = 1.70
IE₅ / IE₄ = 1.23
IE₆ / IE₅ = 3.54 ← largest ratio
Explanation

Reading the answer

01
Sudden increase in ionisation energy from IE₅ to IE₆.
02
The 6th electron is removed from the inner shell which has stable noble gas electron configuration and closer to the nucleus.
03
Much greater energy needed to remove the 6th electron.
04
Therefore, valence electrons = 5, block p, and Group 15.
05
Valence electronic configuration: ns² np³.
The single rule to remember

Number of valence electrons = position of the IE just before the drastic jump. If the jump is between IE₃ and IE₄, there are 3 valence electrons → Group 2 (if s-block) or Group 13 (if p-block). Use the configuration to confirm which block.

15 Summary

What you should walk away with

Ionisation energy is the central numerical quantity for "how tightly atoms hold their electrons." Four reasoning patterns to take with you:

  • DEFNIE₁: minimum energy to remove 1 mol of e⁻ from 1 mol of gaseous atoms
  • ACROSSIE increases — effective nuclear charge stronger, more energy needed
  • DOWNIE decreases — shielding stronger, less energy needed
  • JUMPDrastic jump in successive IE = where you cross into a new (inner) shell
How this shows up in PSPM

Part B is mandatory for all students and almost always features a successive IE table or graph. The standard task: given the IE data, deduce the group, valence configuration, and full electron configuration. The PSPM mark scheme rewards the keyword sequence: "sudden increase from IEₙ to IEₙ₊₁ ... nth electron removed from inner shell ... stable noble gas configuration ... closer to the nucleus ... much greater energy needed." Use this exact language — every phrase is a mark.

The Al/Mg and S/P anomalies appear in Q3 of every CA set. Remember: fully filled and half filled are more stable than partially filled.
