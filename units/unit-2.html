02 Before You Begin

A quick check on prior knowledge

This unit assumes you can already write the electron configuration of an atom and its ion. Three rules from Unit 1 and Topic 2 you must be comfortable with:

For main group cations, electrons are removed from the outermost shell to achieve a noble-gas configuration. For example, Al → Al³⁺ means removing the 3s² 3p¹ electrons.

For main group anions, electrons are added to the outermost shell to achieve a noble-gas configuration. For example, O → O²⁻ means adding 2 electrons to the 2p subshell.

For transition metal cations, electrons are always removed first from the ns orbital, then from the (n−1)d orbital. For example, Fe → Fe²⁺ means removing the 4s² electrons first, giving [Ar] 3d⁶.

03 Three Factors

Three factors that influence size

Before explaining any trend in the periodic table, you need to understand three factors. Every PSPM answer about atomic or ionic radii will be built from these three:

The three factors that determine atomic and ionic size
The distinction

Nuclear charge is the positive charge felt by all electrons (equals the proton number). Effective nuclear charge is the positive charge felt by the valence electrons only, calculated as Zeff = proton number − number of inner electrons.

For example, sodium has a proton number of 11. Its nuclear charge is +11, felt by all 11 electrons. But its effective nuclear charge is only +1, because the 10 inner electrons shield the single valence electron from feeling the full +11.

04 Atomic Radii Trends

How atomic radius changes across the table

The atomic radius is half the distance between the two nuclei of a diatomic molecule. It changes systematically as you move across the periodic table:

Atomic size decreases across a period and increases down a group
05 Across a Period

Atomic radius decreases across a period

Across a period (from left to right), the atomic radius generally decreases. The PSPM-style explanation uses this exact reasoning chain — learn it word for word:

PSPM Reasoning Chain · Across a Period

Why Al is smaller than Na

01
As proton number increases, but the number of inner electrons remains the same.
02
Effective nuclear charge increases.
03
Positive charge felt by valence electron toward nucleus is stronger.
04
Attraction of nucleus toward valence electron is stronger.
05
Valence electrons move closer to the nucleus.
06
Atomic radius smaller.
Mnemonic from your notes — PIECES

Proton number increases · Inner electrons remain the same · Effective nuclear charge stronger · Attracts valence electrons more strongly · Electrons move closer to the nucleus · Shrink — smaller atomic radius.

06 Down a Group

Atomic radius increases down a group

Going down a group, the atomic radius generally increases. This time the dominant factor is shielding effect, not effective nuclear charge:

PSPM Reasoning Chain · Down a Group

Why K is bigger than Li

01
Principal quantum number, n, increases.
02
Number of inner electrons increases.
03
Inner electrons shield valence electrons strongly.
04
Shielding effect increases.
05
Attraction of nucleus toward valence electron is weaker.
06
Valence electrons move apart from the nucleus.
07
Atomic radius bigger.
07 Transition Metals

The first row of transition elements — less uniform

Across the first row of transition elements (Sc to Zn in Period 4), the trend is less uniform. It splits into three regions:

Sc to Cr — atomic radius decreases

Proton number increases, resulting in an increase in nuclear charge. Attraction of nucleus toward electrons getting stronger. Electrons are pulled closer to the nucleus, making the radius getting smaller from Sc to Cr.

Mn to Ni — approximately constant

Electrons are filled to the inner shell (3d) but the number of electrons in the 4s orbital remains the same. The increased nuclear charge is partly cancelled by the increased screening effect of electrons in the 3d-orbitals of the penultimate shell. This makes the radius not change much among Mn, Fe, Co, and Ni.

Cu and Zn — atomic radius increases

All 5 orbitals in 3d are full with 10 electrons. Electron-electron repulsion in the fully-filled 3d orbital increases. The repulsive forces from the paired electrons in 3d orbitals become dominant compared with nuclear charge. Electrons in 4s orbital push apart from electrons in 3d orbital, causing expansion of the electron cloud between 4s and 3d orbital. This results in the atomic size at Cu and Zn increasing.

09 Atom vs Ion

What happens to size when an atom becomes an ion?

When an atom becomes an ion, its size changes — and the direction depends on whether it forms a cation or an anion. Two reasoning chains, each one a PSPM standard:

PSPM Reasoning Chain · Atom → Cation

Why a cation is smaller than its neutral atom

01
Cation is formed when its atom donates/removes outermost electron(s) to achieve a stable electronic configuration.
02
Number of electrons in the cation is lesser compared to its neutral atom.
03
Electron-electron repulsion / mutual repulsion of electrons in the cation is weaker.
04
Attraction between nucleus toward valence electrons in the cation is stronger than its atom.
05
Valence electrons in the cation move closer to the nucleus.
06
Therefore, the size of the cation is smaller than its neutral atom.
PSPM Reasoning Chain · Atom → Anion

Why an anion is bigger than its neutral atom

01
Anion is formed when its atom accepts/gains electron(s) to achieve a stable electronic configuration.
02
Number of electrons in the anion is more compared to its neutral atom.
03
Electron-electron repulsion / mutual repulsion of electrons in the anion is stronger.
04
Attraction between nucleus toward valence electrons in the anion is weaker than its atom.
05
Valence electrons in the anion move apart from the nucleus.
06
Therefore, the size of the anion is bigger than its neutral atom.
PSPM Tip

The key phrase in both chains is "electron-electron repulsion" (or "mutual repulsion of electrons"). This is the distinguishing factor when comparing an atom to its own ion — the proton number doesn't change, so effective nuclear charge stays the same. What changes is the number of electrons, and therefore the repulsion between them.

11 Isoelectronic Species

What does "isoelectronic" mean?

Definition

Atoms or ions are called isoelectronic when they have the same number of electrons and the same electronic configuration.

For example, the species N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, and Al³⁺ are all isoelectronic. Each has 10 electrons in the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ — the same as a neon atom.

Among isoelectronic species, the size depends entirely on the proton number. The species with the most positive charge is the smallest. The species with the most negative charge is the largest.

PSPM Reasoning Chain · Isoelectronic Radii

Why N³⁻ is bigger than Al³⁺

01
All the ions have the same total of 10 electrons. Electronic configuration: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶.
02
Decreasing proton number from Al³⁺ to N³⁻, but the number of inner electrons remains the same.
03
Effective nuclear charge also decreases significantly.
04
The attraction of the nucleus towards the valence electrons getting weaker.
05
Valence electrons pulled apart from the nucleus.
06
Ionic size getting bigger from Al³⁺ to N³⁻.
Key insight

For isoelectronic species, the reasoning uses effective nuclear charge — because all the species share the same number of electrons and the same configuration, the only thing varying is the proton number. This is different from the atom-vs-ion case, where electron-electron repulsion is the key.

13 Ionic Radii Across Period 2

Ionic radii across Period 2 — the drastic jump

When you look at the ionic radii across Period 2, the trend isn't smooth — it has a drastic/sudden increase in the middle, where the elements switch from forming cations to forming anions.

From Li⁺ to C⁴⁺ — ionic radius decreases

Li⁺, Be²⁺, B³⁺, C⁴⁺ are isoelectronic species (1s²) which have similar electronic configuration. From Li⁺ to C⁴⁺, the number of protons increases but the number of inner electrons remains the same. Thus, effective nuclear charge increases. Attraction of nucleus toward valence electrons getting stronger. Valence electrons move closer to the nucleus. Ionic size decreases.

From C⁴⁺ to N³⁻ — a drastic increase

There is a drastic/sudden increase in ionic radius from C⁴⁺ to N³⁻. N³⁻ ion has a higher principal quantum number / extra shell for valence electrons (n=2) compared to C⁴⁺ (n=1). N³⁻ ion has more inner electrons. Hence, the shielding effect in N³⁻ ion is greater than in C⁴⁺ ion. Thus, attraction of nucleus toward valence electron in N³⁻ ion is weaker than in C⁴⁺ ion. Valence electrons move apart from the nucleus in N³⁻. Ionic size N³⁻ is bigger than C⁴⁺.

From N³⁻ to F⁻ — ionic radius decreases again

N³⁻, O²⁻, F⁻ are isoelectronic species (1s² 2s² 2p⁶) which have similar electronic configuration. From N³⁻ to F⁻, the number of protons increases but the number of inner electrons remains the same. Thus, effective nuclear charge increases. Attraction of nucleus toward valence electrons getting stronger. Valence electrons move closer to the nucleus. Ionic size decreases.

The pattern across Period 2 ions

Li⁺ > Be²⁺ > B³⁺ > C⁴⁺ (cations: isoelectronic, decreasing size)
then drastic jump up
N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ (anions: isoelectronic, decreasing size)

What you should walk away with

  • ACROSSEffective nuclear charge increases → smaller atomic radius
  • DOWNShielding effect increases → bigger atomic radius
  • ATOM↔IONElectron-electron repulsion change → cation smaller, anion bigger
  • ISO-ESame electrons; proton number rules → more +charge = smaller
