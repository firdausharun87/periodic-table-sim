03 Defining Electronegativity

What is electronegativity?

Definition · Electronegativity

The tendency for an atom to attract electrons to itself when it is chemically combined with another element.

Atoms with strong attraction for the bonding electrons have high electronegativity. Consider the H–F bond as an example:

In H–F, electrons spend more time closer to F (higher electronegativity)

The electrons spend more time closer to F. F is more electronegative than H. This causes the F end of the bond to be partially negative (δ⁻) and the H end partially positive (δ⁺). H–F has a polar bond.

The benchmark

The highest electronegativity value (4.0) is assigned to fluorine. All other elements are measured relative to it on the Pauling scale.

04 Variation Trends

How electronegativity varies across the table

Electronegativity follows the same general trend as first ionisation energy — and for the same underlying reasons. The smaller the atom and the stronger the effective nuclear charge, the more strongly it pulls on bonding electrons.

06 Bond Type

From electronegativity to bond type

Electronegativity is the bridge between atomic structure and chemical bonding. The difference in electronegativity between two atoms tells you what kind of bond they form:

Electronegativity difference determines bond type

This is the bridge to the next big idea: the type of bond an element forms with oxygen determines whether its oxide is basic, amphoteric, or acidic.

07 Period 3 Oxides

The acid-base character of Period 3 oxides

When Period 3 elements react with oxygen, the resulting oxides show a clear pattern that follows electronegativity. Walking from left to right across Period 3:

Acid-base character of Period 3 oxides — the transition from basic to acidic
The three-part pattern

When Period 3 elements react with oxygen: metals form basic oxides, nonmetals form acidic oxides, and some metals and metalloids form amphoteric oxides (showing both acidic and basic behaviour).

08 Basic Oxides

Metal oxides — ionic and basic

Na₂O and MgO are metal oxides. They are ionic compounds because the large electronegativity difference between metal and oxygen leads to electron transfer. In water, they act as bases — producing metal hydroxide and reacting with acids to form salt and water.

Basic Oxide Reactions

Na₂O and MgO

→
Na₂O reacts with water:
Na₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq)
basic oxide → base
→
MgO reacts with acid:
MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
basic oxide → salt + water
09 Amphoteric Oxides

Aluminium oxide — both acidic and basic

Al₂O₃ is the classic amphoteric oxide — it can react with both acid and base. This dual behaviour reflects aluminium's position at the borderline between metals and nonmetals. Al₂O₃ has bonding that's intermediate between ionic and covalent.

Amphoteric Oxide Reactions · Al₂O₃

Both equations needed for full marks

→
With acid (HCl) — acts as a base:
Al₂O₃(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂O(l)
amphoteric oxide reacting with acid → salt + water
→
With base (NaOH) — acts as an acid:
Al₂O₃(s) + 2NaOH(aq) → 2NaAlO₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
amphoteric oxide reacting with base → sodium aluminate + water
PSPM trap

When asked about Al₂O₃, you must write both reactions — with acid AND with base. Writing only one earns only half the marks. The mark scheme specifically checks that students demonstrate understanding of amphoteric behaviour, which requires showing both directions.

11 Acidic Oxides

Nonmetal oxides — covalent and acidic

SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₃, and Cl₂O₇ are nonmetal oxides. They are covalent compounds because the electronegativity difference between nonmetal and oxygen is small. In water, they act as acids — producing H⁺ ions and reacting with bases to form salt and water.

Acidic Oxide Reactions

Nonmetal oxides + water → acids

→
SO₃ + water:
SO₃(g) + H₂O(l) → H₂SO₄(aq)
acidic oxide → sulphuric acid
→
P₄O₁₀ + water:
P₄O₁₀(s) + 6H₂O(l) → 4H₃PO₄(aq)
acidic oxide → phosphoric acid
→
Cl₂O₇ + water:
Cl₂O₇(s) + H₂O(l) → 2HClO₄(aq)
acidic oxide → perchloric acid
Note on SiO₂

SiO₂ is an acidic oxide but it is insoluble in water. It does not react with water directly, but it does react with strong bases — confirming its acidic nature.

12 Linking Bonding to Behaviour

Why the transition basic → amphoteric → acidic?

The pattern across Period 3 is not arbitrary — it follows directly from electronegativity difference between the element and oxygen:

The Reasoning Chain · Why oxides change character

From metal to nonmetal, bonding changes — and so does acid-base behaviour

01
Left side (Na, Mg): Low electronegativity. Large electronegativity difference with oxygen → ionic bonding → metal oxides → basic.
02
Middle (Al): Intermediate electronegativity. Moderate electronegativity difference with oxygen → intermediate bonding (partly ionic, partly covalent) → amphoteric.
03
Right side (Si, P, S, Cl): High electronegativity. Small electronegativity difference with oxygen → covalent bonding → nonmetal oxides → acidic.
The full PSPM answer

When asked to "explain the acid-base character of Period 3 oxides," your answer must link bonding type to acid-base behaviour. Don't just list which oxides are basic and which are acidic — explain why using the electronegativity → bond type → oxide character chain. This is what earns full marks.

15 Summary

What you should walk away with

You've now connected three layers of periodic table theory — atomic structure, electronegativity, and chemical behaviour. The big takeaways:

  • DEFNElectronegativity: tendency of atom to attract bonding electrons
  • TRENDIncreases across, decreases down — same logic as IE
  • BONDBig EN difference → ionic; small EN difference → covalent
  • OXIDEBasic (metal) → Amphoteric (Al₂O₃) → Acidic (nonmetal)
How this shows up in PSPM

Q3 of every CA set asks you to predict the acid-base character of given oxides, write balanced equations for their reactions with water (or with acid/base for amphoteric Al₂O₃), and explain the pattern across Period 3 using electronegativity and bonding. The mark scheme rewards the full reasoning chain: "electronegativity increases → EN difference with O decreases → bond type changes from ionic to covalent → oxide character changes from basic to acidic, with Al₂O₃ amphoteric at the borderline."

Always write both equations for Al₂O₃ — with acid AND with base — when asked to demonstrate amphoteric behaviour.
